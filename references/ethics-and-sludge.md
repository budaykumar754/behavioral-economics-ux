# Ethics and sludge

Behavioral economics describes how people actually decide. It does not give permission to exploit that.

## Helpful nudge vs sludge

| Helpful | Sludge |
|---------|--------|
| Removes a real barrier to a goal the user already has | Adds friction to a goal the user has (cancel, compare, leave) |
| Makes costs and commitment visible before action | Hides price, term, or difficulty until after action |
| Uses real social proof from similar users | Invents counts, "others are looking", fake timers |
| Defaults that match most people's informed preference | Defaults that serve the company against typical preference |
| Prompts when ability and motivation are high | Prompts that interrupt, shame, or fatigue |
| Easy to reverse | Easy to start, hard to stop |

If users would feel tricked when they notice the tactic, it is sludge.

NN/G and adjacent research treat "clean the sludge from decision-making workflows" as part of the same practice as applying behavioral economics. Use both directions: add support for the target behavior *and* remove sludge from exit, comparison, and refusal paths.

## Required ethics check before recommending an intervention

1. Does the target behavior serve a goal the user already expressed or revealed through action?
2. Would you be comfortable explaining the tactic in plain language on the screen where it runs?
3. Is reversal as easy as initiation (cancel, undo, export, opt out)?
4. Are money, time, data, and lock-in disclosed before the commitment click?
5. What is the counter metric that would show we harmed people (refunds, chargebacks, support rage, regret surveys, rapid churn after conversion)?

If any answer is no, redesign or refuse.

## Patterns to refuse

- Fake urgency or scarcity
- Confirmshaming ("No thanks, I hate saving money")
- Roach motels (easy in, hard out)
- Hidden subscriptions and forced continuity
- Bait-and-switch pricing
- Privacy-Zuckering (tricking people into oversharing)
- Disguised ads and fake UI chrome
- Nagging prompts after a clear no

Do not rename these as "loss aversion" or "social proof" and ship them.

## How to argue this to a stakeholder

Frame it as risk, not vibe:

- Short-term conversion from sludge often raises refunds, chargebacks, app-store complaints, and brand damage.
- Users who convert under pressure are worse customers than users who convert under clarity.
- The same frameworks that increase completion also increase satisfaction when they remove friction instead of adding pressure.

Propose an A/B that measures both completion and regret, not completion alone.
